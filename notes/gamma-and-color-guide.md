# What the LED emits and what the eye sees

*A from-first-principles guide to color on an addressable LED: emitted light against perceived brightness, the gamma table between them, why the library holds sixteen bits and sends eight, where a brightness knob belongs, and HSV in integers.*

This is a teaching document, not project documentation. It explains the one fact that most color code on LED strips either gets right on purpose or wrong by accident — the LED is linear in light and the eye is not — and follows it through every stage between a `Color` and the byte on the wire. The running example is this repository's color path: `Color::lerp()` and `Color::hsv()` in `include/stripseq/color.h`, and the gamma table and `to_bytes()` in `src/color.cpp`. It assumes [`integer-arithmetic-guide.md`](integer-arithmetic-guide.md) §1 and §4 for why every product starts with a `uint32_t` cast, and its §7 if you want to know how the table is computed without floating point. Beyond that, only C++ itself.

**The shape of this document:**

- **§1–§2** — the two halves of the problem: what the LED emits for a byte, and what the eye makes of it.
- **§3** — the gamma table that joins them, and what it costs at the dark end.
- **§4** — why the library holds sixteen bits a channel and sends eight, and exactly what the table gives up by throwing the extra eight away.
- **§5** — where a brightness knob belongs, and the trade that no order escapes.
- **§6** — which space a blend happens in, and what that does to a fade's midpoint.
- **§7** — HSV as a hexagon walked in integers.
- **§8** — the synthesis: the whole path from a `Color` to the eye.
- **§9** — six exercises.
- **§10–§11** — the model in a paragraph, and where to go next.

## 1. What the LED emits

A WS2812-style pixel takes eight bits per channel and drives each emitter with a fixed current, switched on and off many hundreds of times a second. The byte sets the duty cycle: 255 is on all the time, 128 is on half the time, 1 is on for 1/255 of it. The eye averages the flicker, so what it receives is light in proportion to the byte. **On the wire, the byte is linear in emitted light.**

That is the convenient half. Light from the three emitters adds, too: a pixel at (255, 128, 0) emits full red light and half green light, and the mix is what reaches you. Every statement in this guide about "the ratio of green to red" is a statement about those emitted amounts, because the ratio is what decides the hue you see.

## 2. What the eye sees

The other half is inconvenient. Perceived brightness is not proportional to light. It is roughly a power of it — CIE lightness is close to a cube root over most of its range — so the eye is very sensitive to changes in dim light and much less sensitive to the same change in bright light. Going from 1 to 2 doubles the light, and is a step anyone can see. Going from 254 to 255 adds the same amount of light, and nobody can see it.

Put the two halves together and a ramp that is even in bytes is uneven to the eye. A fade from 255 down to 0 in equal steps spends most of its time looking almost full and then collapses: the last handful of steps, 4 → 3 → 2 → 1 → 0, are each large jumps in perceived brightness. This is the "snaps off near the bottom" that `docs/DESIGN.md` describes, and it is the most visible quality difference there is on a strip, because every fade to black ends in it.

## 3. The table between them

The fix is to stop writing colors in the space the LED speaks. Colors are written in a space that is roughly even to the eye, and a curve maps each channel to the light it should become just before it leaves:

```
emitted = 255 · (written / 255) ^ 2.5
```

A written value of 128, "half as bright to the eye", becomes an emitted byte of 46 — about 18 % of the light, which is what looks like half. The exponent undoes the eye's power law, approximately: 2.2 is the sRGB-ish value, 2.8 is a popular choice for LEDs, and this library uses 2.5. It sits between them, and it is the one of the three that can be computed exactly in integer arithmetic, which this library requires even at compile time — see [`integer-arithmetic-guide.md`](integer-arithmetic-guide.md) §7. The curve is a 256-entry `uint8_t` table, generated by `make_gamma_table()` in `src/color.cpp`.

The curve has a price, and it is all at the dark end. It is steep near the top and nearly flat near the bottom, so many written values crowd into the first few emitted bytes (§9, Exercise 1):

| Exponent | Distinct outputs, of 256 | Lit inputs that emit nothing | 128 emits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1.0 (no table) | 256 | 0 | 128 |
| 2.2 | 184 | 14 | 56 |
| 2.5 | 173 | 21 | 46 |
| 2.8 | 163 | 27 | 37 |

Inputs 1 to 21 all emit 0 under 2.5. That is not the table being wasteful: emitted byte 1 is the dimmest light the pixel can make, and those written values ask for less. **The bottom of a perceptual scale is below the bottom of an 8-bit linear one**, and no curve can put light there. A higher exponent spreads the visible range more evenly to the eye and loses more of the darkest levels to doing so.

`STRIPSEQ_GAMMA=0` removes the table. Then written values *are* emitted light, divided by 256, and fades snap off again — which is a fair choice for an application that wants the bytes it wrote, and the reason the table is optional.

## 4. Sixteen bits in, eight out

Inside the library a channel is sixteen bits; on the wire it is eight. The extra eight bits look like they should buy more output levels, and they cannot: the pixel takes a byte, so at most 256 levels leave the library whatever happens inside it.

They are for **arithmetic**, not output. A fade that runs for a minute computes a color every frame, each a blend of two others, and in a ColorFn a blend of blends. At eight bits every one of those rounds to a whole level, the errors accumulate, and a slow fade moves in visible stairs. At sixteen the rounding happens 256 times finer, and only once at the end does the value meet the wire's resolution. `Color::rgb8()` scales by 257 rather than shifting by eight so that 255 widens to exactly 65535 — the "exact at both ends" argument from [`integer-arithmetic-guide.md`](integer-arithmetic-guide.md) §5, applied to widening.

So the conversion spends the extra bits by design. The gamma table is indexed by the **top byte** of the channel, and that does cost something, which is worth stating exactly. The 256 indices reach 173 distinct emitted bytes (§3). A table indexed by all sixteen bits would reach all 256: the 83 it skips are 17 at the dark end and 66 in the bright half, where the curve is steep and one written level spans two or three emitted bytes — 250, 253, 255 at the very top. What those bytes would add is steps finer than one written level, and a written level is already an even 1/255 to the eye, so the table caps the strip at 256 even-looking levels rather than at 256 emitted bytes. Reaching the rest would take a 64 KB table, or interpolation between entries for every channel of every pixel. `convert()` and `encode()` in `src/color.cpp` are the whole of the cheap version: shift, then look up.

## 5. Where the brightness knob goes

A strip has a brightness level, 0 to 255, applied to everything on it. There are two places to apply it, and they are not equivalent.

**Before the curve:** scale the written value, `v · level / 255`, then look it up. The library computes the scaling as `(v · (level + 1)) >> 16`, which is the top byte of the division or one above it, at the cost of no division at all ([`integer-arithmetic-guide.md`](integer-arithmetic-guide.md) §6). Because the written space is even to the eye, a level of 128 looks half as bright, and the knob feels even all the way down.

**After the curve:** look the value up, then scale the emitted byte. Emitted bytes are light, so a level of 128 halves the light — which looks about 76 % as bright, because the eye compresses the change (§2). The knob does almost nothing across its top half and all its work near the bottom.

That would settle it, except for what happens at the dim end. A knob even to the eye asks for very little light at low settings: level 32 asks for (32/255)^2.5 of full light, under 0.6 %, which for most channels is less than one emitted byte. So each channel rounds down to 0 at its own setting, the weakest one first, and the ratio between channels — the hue — drifts before the color goes out. An orange shows it (§9, Exercise 2):

| Level | Green / red light, before the curve | After the curve |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 255 | 0.180 | 0.180 |
| 128 | 0.174 | 0.180 |
| 64 | 0.125 | 0.172 |
| 32 | red only | 0.156 |

And below level 22, even full white is black, because 22 is the first written value the curve lights (§3). Scaling after the curve keeps the hue to much lower settings, because it divides the light directly and keeps it in the upper bytes for longer — and gives up the even knob to do it.

Neither order escapes this. **A knob that is even to the eye needs light levels finer than one byte at the dim end, and the wire does not have them.** The order decides which property gives way. This library applies brightness before the curve, so the knob is even and the dim end is coarse; the comment on `to_bytes()` in `src/color_internal.h` records that choice beside its cost. Without the table the two orders differ only in rounding.

## 6. Which space a blend happens in

`Color::lerp()` blends each channel in the written space, before the curve. With gamma on, that space is roughly even to the eye, so a fade from black to white reaches its midpoint looking half as bright, which is what a fade should do. Its midpoint emits only 18 % of the light.

That is not how light mixes physically. Two lights at half power each, overlapped, emit their average; a physically correct blend would happen after the curve, in emitted light, and would look too bright too soon. For colors on a strip — fades, ramps, gradients a person watches — blending where the eye is even is the useful choice, and it is free, because the written space is where the values already are. A ColorFn that calls `lerp()` inherits it automatically. In a build without the table the written space *is* light, and a blend is the physical one.

## 7. HSV as a hexagon in integers

Hue is the easiest way to describe a rainbow, and `Color::hsv(hue, sat, val)` converts it with no floating point. Picture the color wheel as a hexagon with red, yellow, green, cyan, blue and magenta at its corners. Walking around it, there is always:

- one channel at the value,
- one at the floor that saturation leaves, `val · (255 − sat) / 255`,
- and one ramping between them, rising on the way into a corner and falling on the way out.

Which channel does which changes at each corner, so the hue splits into a **sextant** — which of the six edges — and a **position** along it. `hue · 6` does both at once: its top bits are the sextant, 0 to 5, and its low sixteen are the position, 0 to 65535 within the edge. Six `case`s then place the three channels. It all fits `uint32_t` with room to spare, and the function is `constexpr`, so a palette of hues costs no code at startup.

Two details are worth knowing because they look like bugs and are not:

- **Only two corners can be hit exactly.** A corner is at a multiple of 65536 / 6, which is not a whole number. Hue 0 is pure red and hue 0x8000 is pure cyan; the other four fall between two hues, so 0x2AAA, the cookbook's yellow, comes out with green at 65532 instead of 65535 — the same top byte, so the same color on the wire.
- **`hue · 6` is always even**, so the position stops at 65534 and an edge ends a step short of where the next begins. Combined with each hue step moving the position by 6, no channel moves by more than 7 per hue anywhere around the wheel, corners and the wrap from 65535 back to 0 included.

That bound is the test. A one-off at a corner is invisible in a still gradient and obvious as a flicker in a rotating rainbow, so `tests/color.cpp` walks all 65,536 hues and checks the largest jump, rather than checking a few colors it thought to pick (§9, Exercise 4).

In an RGBW build, `hsv()` leaves white at 0. Deciding how a hue maps onto a fourth emitter is the same policy question as deriving white from RGB, and `docs/DESIGN.md` explains why the library answers neither.

## 8. The whole path

From a color to the eye, in order:

| Stage | Where | Space | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Write a color | `Color::rgb()`, `hsv()`, a palette | written, even to the eye | 16 bits a channel, so arithmetic stays fine-grained (§4) |
| Blend | `Color::lerp()`, every fade | written | midpoints look halfway (§6) |
| Brightness | `to_bytes()` | written | even knob; coarse at the dim end (§5) |
| Drop eight bits | `convert()` | written, 8-bit | spends the precision kept for arithmetic (§4) |
| Gamma | the table | written → emitted | undoes the eye's power law (§3) |
| The wire | the driver | emitted | the byte becomes a duty cycle (§1) |
| The eye | — | perceived | roughly the cube root of the light (§2) |

Everything above the gamma row is arithmetic on values that mean "how bright should this look", and everything below it is light. The table is the one place the two meet, and every decision in this guide is about which side of it something belongs on.

### Exercise 1 — What the curve throws away

*Demonstrates §3.*

```cpp
// curve.cpp
#include <cmath>
#include <cstdio>
#include <set>

int main() {
    for (double exponent : {1.0, 2.2, 2.5, 2.8}) {
        std::set<long> distinct;
        int dark = 0, first_lit = -1;
        for (int in = 0; in < 256; ++in) {
            const long out = std::lround(255.0 * std::pow(in / 255.0, exponent));
            distinct.insert(out);
            if (in > 0 && out == 0) ++dark;
            if (out > 0 && first_lit < 0) first_lit = in;
        }
        std::printf("gamma %.1f: %3zu distinct outputs, %2d lit inputs go dark, first lit at %3d, "
                    "half (128) emits %3ld\n",
                    exponent, distinct.size(), dark, first_lit,
                    std::lround(255.0 * std::pow(128 / 255.0, exponent)));
    }
}
```

```sh
c++ -std=c++20 -O2 curve.cpp -o curve && ./curve
```

```
gamma 1.0: 256 distinct outputs,  0 lit inputs go dark, first lit at   1, half (128) emits 128
gamma 2.2: 184 distinct outputs, 14 lit inputs go dark, first lit at  15, half (128) emits  56
gamma 2.5: 173 distinct outputs, 21 lit inputs go dark, first lit at  22, half (128) emits  46
gamma 2.8: 163 distinct outputs, 27 lit inputs go dark, first lit at  28, half (128) emits  37
```

**Proves:** a gamma curve cannot add output levels, only redistribute them, and it pays for an even scale with the darkest written values, which ask for less light than one byte makes. The higher the exponent, the more of them go dark.

### Exercise 2 — Two orders for one knob

*Demonstrates §5.*

```cpp
// order.cpp
#include <cmath>
#include <cstdint>
#include <cstdio>
#include <initializer_list>

// The library's gamma table, by its double definition: emitted byte for a top byte.
unsigned encode(unsigned top) { return (unsigned)std::lround(255.0 * std::pow(top / 255.0, 2.5)); }

// The library: scale the 16-bit channel, then gamma.
unsigned before(unsigned v, unsigned level) { return encode((v * (level + 1)) >> 16); }

// The shortcut: gamma, then scale the emitted byte.
unsigned after(unsigned v, unsigned level) { return encode(v >> 8) * level / 255; }

// How bright an emitted byte looks, 0..1: light to the power 1/2.5.
double looks(unsigned byte) { return std::pow(byte / 255.0, 1 / 2.5); }

int main() {
    const unsigned r = 65535, g = 32768;   // an orange
    std::printf("orange, green/red light ratio (full brightness: %.3f)\n",
                double(encode(g >> 8)) / encode(r >> 8));
    for (unsigned level : {128u, 64u, 32u, 16u}) {
        auto ratio = [](unsigned rr, unsigned gg) { return rr ? double(gg) / rr : 0.0; };
        std::printf("  level %3u  before (%3u,%3u) %.3f   after (%3u,%3u) %.3f\n", level,
                    before(r, level), before(g, level), ratio(before(r, level), before(g, level)),
                    after(r, level), after(g, level), ratio(after(r, level), after(g, level)));
    }
    std::printf("white, how bright it looks\n");
    for (unsigned level : {255u, 128u, 64u, 21u, 22u}) {
        std::printf("  level %3u  before %.2f   after %.2f\n", level,
                    looks(before(65535, level)), looks(after(65535, level)));
    }
}
```

```sh
c++ -std=c++20 -O2 order.cpp -o order && ./order
```

```
orange, green/red light ratio (full brightness: 0.180)
  level 128  before ( 46,  8) 0.174   after (128, 23) 0.180
  level  64  before (  8,  1) 0.125   after ( 64, 11) 0.172
  level  32  before (  1,  0) 0.000   after ( 32,  5) 0.156
  level  16  before (  0,  0) 0.000   after ( 16,  2) 0.125
white, how bright it looks
  level 255  before 1.00   after 1.00
  level 128  before 0.50   after 0.76
  level  64  before 0.25   after 0.58
  level  21  before 0.00   after 0.37
  level  22  before 0.11   after 0.38
```

**Proves:** brightness before the curve makes the knob even to the eye — 128 looks like 0.50 — and pushes every channel into the bottom few bytes to do it, where the hue drifts and the dimmest settings go black. After the curve, the hue holds and the knob is lopsided. Each order buys one property with the other.

### Exercise 4 — A sextant width that does not divide

*Demonstrates §7, and why the test sweeps every hue.*

A natural way to split the hue is to divide by the width of a sextant, rounded up to a whole number: 65536 / 6 is 10922.67, so 10923. In `Color::hsv()` in `include/stripseq/color.h`, replace the two lines that compute `sextant` and `pos` with:

```cpp
        const uint32_t sextant = hue / 10923u;
```

```
  logged: sat := 255
          val := 255
```

### Exercise 5 — An oracle that shares nothing

*Demonstrates the testing argument behind `tests/convert.cpp`; see [`testing-guide.md`](testing-guide.md) §8.*

`make_gamma_table()` in `src/color.cpp` rounds by asking whether `(2n + 1)² · 255³ ≤ 4 · in⁵`. Make the most likely slip and drop the halves, so it floors instead: change the line computing `limit` to `in * in * in * in * in`, and the loop's condition to `(n + 1) * (n + 1) * cube <= limit`.

```sh
cmake --build build --target tests_default && ./build/tests_default -tc='to_bytes*'
```

```
[doctest] test cases:    5 |    2 passed |   3 failed | 114 skipped
[doctest] assertions: 1821 | 1040 passed | 781 failed |
```

**Revert it.** The `static_assert` in `src/color.cpp` still passes — 0 and 255 are right either way — so the only thing that notices is the test computing its expectation in `double`, a different method that meets the same definition.

**Proves:** a test is evidence only if its expected value comes from somewhere else. Had the test read the table, or copied the generator, it would have agreed with the bug at all 256 entries.


## 10. The model in one paragraph

An addressable LED emits light in proportion to its byte, because the byte is a duty cycle, and the eye perceives brightness as roughly a power of light, so an even ramp of bytes looks uneven — almost full for most of a fade, then snapping off at the bottom. The library therefore holds colors in a space that is roughly even to the eye and maps each channel to emitted light at the very end, through a 2.5 gamma curve stored as 256 bytes; the curve redistributes the output levels rather than adding any, and the darkest written values ask for less light than one byte makes, so they emit nothing. Channels are sixteen bits inside so that blends of blends stay accurate, and the extra eight are dropped by design just before the table; indexing by the top byte reaches 173 of the 256 emitted bytes, skipping only steps finer than one written level, which is already an even 1/255 to the eye. A brightness knob placed before the curve is even to the eye but asks for sub-byte light at the dim end, so hue drifts and low settings go black; placed after, it keeps hue and feels lopsided — the wire's resolution forces one or the other, and this library chooses the even knob. Blending happens before the curve too, so a fade's midpoint looks halfway rather than emitting half. HSV is a hexagon: `hue · 6` gives the edge and the position along it, six cases place one channel at the value, one at the floor and one ramping, and because 65536 does not divide by six only red and cyan are exact — which is why the test sweeps every hue and bounds every step, wrap included.
